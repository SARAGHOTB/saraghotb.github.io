Experimental Techniques

Experimental physics connects physical ideas to real materials and devices. Thin-film growth, nanofabrication, and characterization form an interconnected workflow: materials are created, patterned into structures, measured, and then refined based on experimental feedback.

Experimental Workflow

From materials to measurable devices

Experimental condensed matter physics and device research rarely rely on a single technique.

A material may first be grown as a thin film, structurally characterized, patterned through lithography, etched or metallized, and finally measured electrically, magnetically, optically, or at low temperature.

Each step provides information that influences the next one. This feedback loop is central to both scientific research and process engineering.

Core Areas

Three parts of the experimental toolkit

Thin-Film Growth & Deposition

Thin-film techniques allow materials to be grown or deposited with controlled thickness, composition, interfaces, and structure.

These methods are essential for superconducting films, semiconductor devices, dielectric layers, contacts, and multilayer structures.

  • Pulsed Laser Deposition (PLD)
  • RF/DC Magnetron Sputtering
  • Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD)
  • PECVD / CVD
  • E-beam Evaporation
  • Thermal Evaporation

Nanofabrication

Nanofabrication transforms materials into devices by combining lithography, etching, deposition, pattern transfer, and metallization.

These processes define the geometry, interfaces, and dimensions that ultimately determine device behavior.

  • Photolithography
  • Direct-Write Lithography
  • Lift-off
  • RIE / ICP-RIE

Experimental Feedback

Measurement is part of process development

Characterization is not simply something performed after fabrication is complete.

Measurements guide process decisions throughout development. XRD may determine whether a film is suitable for fabrication. SEM can reveal pattern-transfer failure. AFM can identify surface roughness. Electrical measurements can show leakage, contact resistance, or incomplete isolation.

The process then returns to fabrication with new information.

Experimental cycle

Fabricate → Measure → Diagnose → Modify → Repeat

Much of experimental process development is an iterative loop rather than a linear sequence.
