Nanofabrication

Nanofabrication is where materials science, chemistry, plasma physics, lithography, and process engineering come together to turn a design into a functional device. Over the years, I have worked with a range of micro- and nanoscale fabrication techniques, and this page is where I collect both the fundamentals and the practical lessons that matter once a process leaves the textbook and enters the cleanroom.

Why It Matters

A good pattern is only the beginning

Nanofabrication is often described as a sequence of individual processes: coat a resist, expose a pattern, develop it, etch or deposit a material, remove the resist, and repeat. In practice, those steps are strongly coupled.

The resist profile can determine whether lift-off succeeds. The lithography mask can influence the achievable etch profile. Plasma conditions can change sidewall shape, selectivity, and surface condition. Film thickness and conformality can determine whether a later step remains electrically isolated or becomes shorted.

What makes nanofabrication interesting to me is exactly this interaction between physics and process integration. A process rarely fails because one tool simply “does not work.” More often, the final result reflects how multiple steps interact across the complete fabrication flow.

Lithography

Electron-beam lithography

Electron-beam lithography, or EBL, is one of the most versatile techniques for defining nanoscale patterns. A focused electron beam is scanned across an electron-sensitive resist according to a digital pattern. After development, the resist becomes either the final mask or an intermediate layer for etching, deposition, lift-off, or further pattern transfer.

What makes EBL especially useful is not only its resolution, but also the ability to write arbitrary geometries without a physical mask. This makes it well suited to research-scale device fabrication, alignment-sensitive structures, rapid design iteration, and patterns where feature size approaches the limits of optical lithography.

Resolution is a process property

EBL resolution is often associated with the nominal beam size, but the final feature is determined by the entire process. Electron scattering, resist chemistry, resist thickness, exposure dose, development conditions, substrate composition, and local pattern density all influence the dimensions that remain after development.

A tool capable of producing a very small beam therefore does not automatically guarantee that every resist stack or pattern geometry can reproduce the same minimum feature size.

A useful way to think about EBL

The question is not simply: How small can the beam write?

The more useful question is: Can the complete lithography and pattern-transfer process reproduce the required structure reliably?

Positive and negative resists

In a positive resist, the exposed regions become more soluble in the developer and are removed. In a negative resist, exposure causes the written regions to become less soluble, so the exposed material remains.

The choice depends on the application rather than one resist type being universally better. Important considerations include achievable resolution, sensitivity, resist thickness, etch resistance, pattern density, lift-off geometry, process compatibility, and writing time.

Positive resist

Often useful when high resolution and straightforward pattern definition are important. For lift-off, the developed profile and any undercut can strongly influence how cleanly the deposited film separates.

Negative resist

Can be attractive for dense or high-aspect-ratio structures, depending on the chemistry and process. Sensitivity, crosslinking, removal, and etch resistance can all become important tradeoffs.

Dose and process window

Exposure dose determines how much electron energy is delivered to the resist. Too little dose can leave features incompletely developed or undersized, while excessive dose can broaden features, close small gaps, or increase the influence of proximity effects.

In practice, an EBL process is usually developed through dose testing rather than relying on a single nominal value. The goal is not simply to find one dose that works, but to identify a useful process window where the required dimensions remain stable despite small variations in exposure or development.

Proximity effects

Electrons do not deposit all of their energy exactly where the beam enters the resist. Forward scattering broadens the beam as electrons travel through the resist, while electrons that penetrate the substrate can backscatter and expose surrounding regions.

As a result, dense and isolated structures can receive different effective doses even when the nominal writing dose is identical. Proximity effects can alter line width, spacing, corner definition, and the uniformity of repeated features.

This becomes particularly important when a pattern contains regions with very different local densities. Dose correction, geometry adjustment, resist choice, substrate effects, and development conditions may all become part of the optimization.

Development is part of the lithography process

Exposure changes the chemistry of the resist, but development determines which part of that modified resist actually remains on the sample. Developer composition, time, temperature, agitation, rinse conditions, and resist thickness can therefore change the final critical dimension and profile.

I find it more useful to treat exposure and development as one coupled process rather than optimizing them independently.

From my cleanroom experience

I have used EBL for nanoscale device fabrication where the written resist pattern was only one part of the final requirement. The process also had to support subsequent etching, metallization, or lift-off while preserving the intended geometry.

In practice, this means I pay particular attention to resist thickness, dose, feature density, development quality, alignment, and how the resist will behave during the next fabrication step.

Feature broadening

Possible causes include excessive dose, proximity effects, over-development, or a resist stack that is not well matched to the required dimensions.

Incomplete development

Residual resist can come from insufficient exposure, development time, temperature variation, or resist thickness and chemistry.

Poor pattern transfer

A resist image can look good but still fail during etching if the mask is too thin, selectivity is insufficient, or the resist degrades in the plasma.

Lift-off failure

Even well-resolved features can fail during lift-off if deposition bridges the resist sidewall or the resist profile does not provide enough discontinuity.

Optical Patterning

Photolithography and direct-write lithography

EBL is powerful, but it is not automatically the best tool for every layer. When the required feature size allows it, photolithography can provide much higher throughput and is often more practical for larger device structures, contacts, alignment levels, and repeated wafer-scale processing.

In contact and proximity lithography, the relationship between the mask and the wafer becomes important. Resist thickness, exposure dose, focus, mask-to-wafer spacing, substrate reflectivity, feature dimensions, and development conditions can all influence the final pattern.

Direct-write optical lithography provides another useful option when maskless processing and faster design iteration are more important than achieving the smallest possible feature size.

Pattern Transfer

The lithography pattern is not the device

One of the most important lessons in fabrication is that a beautiful resist pattern does not guarantee a good final structure. The pattern still has to survive transfer into another material.

During dry etching, this introduces another set of constraints: mask selectivity, ion directionality, chemical reactivity, chamber condition, loading effects, sidewall passivation, substrate temperature, and surface condition.

Selectivity

The mask must survive long enough to transfer the desired depth into the underlying material. If mask erosion is too fast, the final dimensions can shift or the target layer may not be fully etched.

Sidewall profile

An etch can remove the correct amount of material and still be unusable if the sidewall angle, undercut, bowing, roughness, or footing is outside the process requirement.

Chamber history

Plasma tools are not perfectly isolated from their usage history. Chamber condition, deposits, previous materials, cleaning state, and tool seasoning can influence reproducibility.

Endpoint thinking

Etch time alone is not always enough. Film thickness variation, tool drift, loading, and material differences can change the amount of overetch required for a robust process.

Plasma Etching

RIE and ICP-RIE

Reactive ion etching combines physical ion bombardment with chemically reactive species. The balance between these mechanisms determines etch rate, anisotropy, selectivity, surface damage, and sidewall behavior.

ICP-RIE adds an inductively coupled plasma source, allowing plasma density to be controlled more independently from substrate bias. That extra degree of control can be extremely valuable when trying to separate ion energy from reactive-species density.

Chemistry

Gas chemistry determines which reactive species are available and how volatile the reaction products are.

Ion energy

Bias controls the energy of ions reaching the substrate and can strongly influence anisotropy and physical damage.

Plasma density

Plasma density influences the concentration of ions and radicals available for the etch reaction.

Metallization

Lift-off and deposition geometry

Lift-off looks simple in a process diagram: pattern the resist, deposit a film, and dissolve the resist. In practice, the geometry of the resist profile and the directionality of the deposition are critical.

If deposited material forms a continuous coating along the resist sidewall, the metal on the substrate can become connected to the film sitting on top of the resist. That makes clean lift-off much more difficult and can create fences, flakes, or unwanted metal remnants.

This is why resist undercut, film thickness, deposition angle, conformality, and resist thickness should be considered together when designing a lift-off process.

Process Integration

Where fabrication becomes systems engineering

Individual processes can often be optimized in isolation. Device fabrication becomes much more challenging when tens or hundreds of steps have to work together.

A change that improves one step can create a new problem several steps later. A thicker dielectric may improve isolation but make contact opening more difficult. A more conformal film can improve coverage but make lift-off impossible. A lithography process that resolves a small feature may use a resist that is too thin to survive the subsequent etch.

For me, process integration means thinking beyond the immediate operation and asking how each decision affects the complete device architecture.

Practical Lessons

What cleanroom work teaches that diagrams do not

01

Start with the process history

When something suddenly changes, I first ask what changed: material lot, tool, chamber condition, resist, operator, measurement method, or upstream process.

02

Metrology is part of fabrication

SEM, profilometry, ellipsometry, reflectometry, AFM, and other measurements are not just final characterization tools. They are feedback mechanisms for process development.

03

Reproducibility matters more than one perfect result

A process is not robust because it worked once. A useful process has a sufficiently wide process window to tolerate realistic variation.

04

Question assumptions

Travelers, recipes, tool settings, and previous results are valuable references, but when evidence consistently points in another direction, the assumptions themselves should be tested.
